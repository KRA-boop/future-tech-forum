Blocking a Smash: First, stabilize the racket face, then decide whether to block the shot or return it.

D01 · Beginner · Approx. 25 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

The ability to use a steady racket face to block a controllable return to the net.

Court Practice

  1. Have a partner feed you 3 sets of 6 shots each at half speed. For each set, first designate a specific landing zone along a straight line, and record the reasons for shots going out of bounds or into the net.
  2. Qualification Criteria In the last set, hit at least 4 shots to the front court, and don't let your elbow pull back with the racket face.

Defensive Skills / Blocking Smashes at the Net

Use slower smashes to develop front-court contact and control of short movements, and gradually practice the next move after a defensive play.

Focus on Consistency in Defense

You don’t always have to hit a smash with full force. A net return can change the space your opponent has to cover, but you must also consider the shuttlecock’s speed, where you make contact, and your opponent’s position. As a beginner, start by practicing consistent returns that land back in the court. Make sure you can control the shuttlecock in front of you before adding different options like net returns, flat drives, or lifts.

Ready Position and Reaction

Keep your knees naturally bent and maintain your balance. Position your racket in front of you where you can react quickly; avoid waiting until the shuttlecock is right next to you before raising your racket. Stay relaxed in your ready position, and adjust your forehand or backhand stance based on the direction of the incoming shuttlecock. Use short, concise strokes to return the shuttlecock, and return to your ready position immediately after hitting it. Avoid using a wide swing for every smash; this will give you time to transition to your next shot.

Start with Slow Shots and Fixed Directions

Have a partner feed you shots in a fixed direction at a controlled speed. Start by practicing ten shots that stay in bounds, then shift your focus to a larger area in the front court. Only increase the speed or vary the direction once you’re comfortable. The feeder and the player should maintain a safe distance. If you can no longer keep up, lower the difficulty or take a break; avoid replacing effective practice with a series of forced returns.

Common Problems and Corrections

If the shuttlecock frequently bounces too high, minimize unnecessary racket swings and regain control of the racket face; if the shuttlecock frequently goes into the net, check whether you’re making contact too late or aiming too close to the net. If you lose your balance after hitting the shuttlecock, slow down first to restore the flow between your preparation, contact, and recovery. When evaluating your progress, consider both the quality of your return and whether you’re ready for the next shot.

Keep Reading the Return Even After Defending

Blocking a shot doesn’t necessarily mean you have to rush the net. First, assess the quality of the return, where your opponent made contact, and your partner’s role, then decide how to move. In doubles, agree on defensive zones with your partner beforehand, and gradually practice covering each other’s positions after returning the shuttlecock to avoid both of you chasing the same shuttlecock at the same time.

Self-Assessment After Practice

Can you perform this set of movements consistently? What is the most common mistake? For the next set, what single aspect will you focus on adjusting? Summarize your observations in one sentence, then decide whether to increase the difficulty.

When Blocking a Smash at the Net, the Shuttlecock Often Floats High—Which Adjustment Should You Try First?

Meet the shuttlecock with the racket head in front of your body, minimize the follow-through, and then make a slight adjustment to the racket face. Ask your partner to feed you the shuttlecock at half speed at first, and gradually compare the height at which it clears the net.
