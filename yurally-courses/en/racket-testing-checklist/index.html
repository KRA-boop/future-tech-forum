A Practical Guide to Choosing a Racket: Finding the Right Setup Through Three Rounds of Test Play

G03 · Advanced · Approx. 30 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

You can use a three-round consistency test to compare two rackets.

Court Practice

  1. Start by hitting the shuttlecock from a stationary position, then move around the court to return the shuttlecock, and finally play 5 consecutive rallies. Both players should use the same shuttlecock and the same feeding speed.
  2. Qualification Criteria Compare the results from the three rounds and clearly identify the factor that most influenced performance.

Class Notes · Rackets and Strings

Compare shuttle control, reaction time, and fatigue levels using consistent shot patterns, and use your test-hit records as a basis for your decision.

Start by Writing Down Your Racket Requirements

Before choosing a racket, make a list of whether you primarily play singles or doubles, how often you play each week, your budget, and the issues that currently trouble you the most. For example, slow reaction times on flat drives, difficulty reaching the backcourt with high clears, or fatigue after prolonged practice. Clearly defining your needs is the only way to determine if a new racket will be helpful—don’t base your decision solely on appearance, which professional players use it, or price.

Round 1—Comparing Control

With similar string tension and grip settings, start by practicing serves, net shots, and targeted clear shots—which are easy to repeat. Observe whether the racket face is easy to control and whether the shuttle’s direction matches your expectations. If there is a significant difference in string tension between the two rackets, make a note of this discrepancy to avoid attributing all your sensations to the racket frame.

Round 2—Comparing Reaction and Transition

Incorporate flat drives, return serves, and continuous swings, and observe whether the transition from the previous stroke back to the ready position is smooth. Weight, balance, shaft response, and grip thickness all influence how the racket feels, so a single specification cannot fully determine its suitability. Start by testing at a moderate speed; there’s no need to hit smashes at full power right from the start.

Round 3—Comparing the Feel of Playing Continuously

Feeling powerful for a short time is different from being able to maintain your form throughout an entire match. After trying out a racket, note whether you’re gripping it too tightly, if your swing rhythm is disrupted, and whether you can maintain consistent control of the shuttlecock when fatigued. If you experience discomfort, stop and review your form and the physical strain—don’t treat enduring pain as a necessary part of the racket-selection process.

Keeping a Simple Record of Your Practice Sessions

For each racket, use a five-point scale to rate control, responsiveness, and comfort, then add a line with specific examples—such as whether it’s easier to lift the racket when returning serves, or if you clearly can’t keep up during consecutive rallies. This is a personal comparison tool, not an objective performance ranking. Using the same set of test shots makes comparisons easier than hitting just one or two different shots.

Adjust the String Pattern Next

First, get a feel for the racket itself, then gradually adjust the strings and grip. If you change too many settings at once, it’s hard to tell which one is making a difference. For more information, check out our guides on racket basics and string tension on this site, and be sure to follow the usage limits specified on your equipment.

Self-check

When hitting from a stationary position, I prefer Racket A, but when moving and returning the shuttlecock, Racket B feels more stable. How should I keep track of this?

Record the results of three separate rounds—static shots, movement, and continuous play—separately, then select the priority based on the game situations you typically encounter. Retest under the same conditions if necessary.
