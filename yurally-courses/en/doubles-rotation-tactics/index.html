Offense and Defense Rotation in Doubles: Maintaining Distance from Your Partner, Covering Positions, and Communication

M01 · Beginner · Approx. 25 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

Being able to judge where you and your partner should position yourselves based on the height of the shuttlecock.

Court Practice

  1. Perform 8 sets of “lift the shuttlecock to transition to defense, then drop it low to transition to offense” at a slow pace. On the third shot of each set, call out the area you want to cover.
  2. Qualification Criteria At least 6 pairs should not chase the same shuttlecock at the same time, and each player should be able to identify their respective positions.

First, Understand This Topic

The fluidity of doubles play depends on both partners working together to maintain court coverage. When opponents return a high shot, one player typically moves to the backcourt to attack, while the other stays at the net to block; when forced to defend, both players should position themselves side by side. After the shuttlecock is lobbed high, the backcourt player moves, and the frontcourt player must adjust accordingly—they cannot remain stationary waiting for the next shot.

Practice and Selection Methods

Rotation isn’t about moving in a fixed circle; rather, it involves adjusting based on the quality of the shot, the point of contact, and the likely return. Shots down the middle often cause both players to hesitate; before the match, you can agree that the player on the forehand side or the one moving forward should take priority in handling the shot. After hitting a flat, fast shot, anticipate your opponent’s block or drive on the next shot, and have your partner move into position simultaneously. Pay attention to your distance from each other with every movement to avoid both chasing the same spot at the same time.

Note a Detail You Can Adjust

On the court, communication should focus on short phrases like “I’ll take it,” “yours,” and “switch,” which can help reduce racket collisions and missed shots. After each practice session, identify the most common positioning mistake—such as both players moving back at the same time or waiting for each other in the middle—and deliberately practice correcting it in the next set. Teamwork isn’t about guessing; it’s about shared expectations and repeated adjustments. To see more on-court scenarios, read Classification of Match Tactics。

Self-check

In doubles, both players are defending side by side when their partner hits a low shot that skims the net. When should they switch positions?

First, see if your opponent can only return the shuttlecock from a lower position. When there’s an opportunity to intercept, one player moves forward to prepare, while the other covers the backcourt.
