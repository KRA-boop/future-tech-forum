How to Choose the Right String Tension: Start by Considering Your Swing Speed and Shot Consistency

G02 · Advanced · Approx. 25 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

Being able to isolate the variables related to stringing and record differences in feel when hitting the shuttlecock.

Court Practice

  1. Based on the markings on your racket and your stringing technician’s recommendations, make a note of your current string type, tension, and date. When comparing results next time, change only one of these factors.
  2. Qualification Criteria Be able to thoroughly document a comparison and describe the differences in terms of consistency, errors, or feel.

First, Understand This Topic

A racket with a higher tension may provide more direct feedback, but the sweet spot may also feel more concentrated, placing higher demands on consistency in contact point and power generation. Beginners or players just returning to training who frequently hit the net or have inconsistent timing should start with a setup that’s easier to control. Tension is not a substitute for power, nor does it automatically increase shuttle speed; the strings, frame, and your personal swing all affect how the racket feels.

Practice and Selection Methods

After restringing your racket, test it using three types of shots: the high clear, the flat drive, and the net shot. With the high clear, observe whether the shot naturally reaches the backcourt; with the flat drive, pay attention to whether the return is too hard or lacks crispness; and with the net shot, check whether your contact is consistent. If only one type of shot improves while the others noticeably deteriorate, make a note of it and re-evaluate the tension, string gauge, or your swing technique. Do not change all conditions at the same time.

Note a Detail You Can Adjust

Strings gradually wear out over time due to use, temperature, and impact, and the racket frame isn’t designed to withstand tension exceeding the recommended range for extended periods. Before stringing your racket, check the label on the racket, and after use, note where the strings broke and the approximate number of hours played. If wear consistently occurs in the same spot, it may be related to your contact point or the condition of the string bed. Don’t simply copy a friend’s string tension just because they’re used to it. Self-check

I changed both the strings and the tension, and the feel has changed. How can I figure out which factor is responsible?

Start with your original setup as a baseline, and within the recommended range for your racket, change either the string type or tension one at a time. Keep track of where the shuttle lands and how it feels during each set.
