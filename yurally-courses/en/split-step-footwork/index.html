When You Return to the Court, Don’t Rush to Smash—Use Your Approach Step to Regain Your Rhythm

B01 · Beginner · Approx. 20 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

Be able to take an approach step in sync with your opponent’s rhythm.

Court Practice

  1. Have your partner randomly call "left" or "right." Start by doing 2 sets of 8 empty-handed movements each, then add the racket.
  2. Qualification Criteria Out of 16 attempts, at least 12 should have the correct direction on the first step, and you should be able to move out again immediately after returning to your position.

When Starting Your Step, It's Not About Jumping as High as Possible

The purpose of the approach step is to keep your body flexible before your opponent hits the shuttlecock, so you can quickly take your first step once the shuttlecock’s trajectory becomes clear. It’s not about jumping as high as possible, nor is it about guessing where your opponent will hit the shuttlecock; excessive movement increases the time it takes to land, and keeping your body too upright makes it difficult to change direction. Focusing on making the step light, short, and ready to transition immediately to the next movement is the key for beginners to practice.

Breaking Down Practice into Three Key Steps

Start by standing near the center of the court with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, and your weight on the shuttles of your feet. When your opponent swings their racket, take a small step to the side; after your foot lands, assess the direction before moving. Ask a partner to use hand signals to indicate forward, backward, left, or right. Practice for 10 to 15 seconds at a time, rest, and repeat for three to five sets. Once you’re comfortable with fixed directions, incorporate random commands and shots to avoid simply memorizing the movements without learning to make judgments.

Common Mistakes and Corrections

If the shuttlecock makes a loud thud when it hits the ground, try lowering the height of your jump; if you always step in the same direction, ask a partner to delay their cue to see if you’re anticipating the shuttlecock too early. Keep breathing during practice, and stop if you feel discomfort in your knees or ankles. At the end of each set, reflect on whether your first step was decisive and whether you maintained your balance during the return, and note the direction where you’re most often a split second too slow. Self-check

Why Do I Still Tend to Be a Step Behind Even Though My Approach Step Is High?

Landing too late will delay your first step. Keep your movements compact, and practice landing lightly just before or after your partner hits the shuttlecock, ensuring you can immediately push off toward the incoming shuttlecock after landing.
