Observing the First Three Shots in Doubles: Reading the Transition from Offense to Defense Based on the Serve

M02 · Advanced · Approx. 30 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

The ability to determine who gains the higher hitting position during the first three rallies.

Court Practice

  1. Select 10 serve-and-return sequences from a full doubles replay, and note the landing spot of the second shot and whether the third shot forced the opponent to lift the shuttlecock.
  2. Qualification Criteria Be able to list two options for returning a serve, along with the subsequent positioning required for each.

Choose a Full BWF Replay to Practice

Class Notes · Practice and Observations

Use three key moments—the serve, the return, and the third shot—to practice understanding the court space and positioning in doubles.

Issues to Be Addressed in This Lesson

When watching doubles matches, if you focus solely on the shuttlecock, it’s easy to overlook how the serve and return have altered the players’ positions. In this lesson, we’ll break down the beginning of a rally into the serve, the return, and the third shot, observing which player achieves a higher hitting position first and which player is forced to step back or lift the shuttlecock. This is a method for learning to read the game; it does not mean there is only one correct way to play every rally.

The First Shot—First, Consider the Limitations and Options

Observe the height and direction of the serve, as well as your opponent’s preparatory stance. A short serve often forces the receiver to make a quick decision at the net, while the server must also be ready to follow up with the next shot. The BWF TV video below features Lin Dan demonstrating his backhand serve technique; it’s a good resource for observing his preparatory movements and shuttle contact. This site uses it as a technical supplement and does not treat a single demonstration as a complete doubles strategy.

The Second Shot—Movement Based on the Landing Spot of the Return

After returning a serve, don’t rush to judge whether it was a good or bad shot. Instead, note where the shuttlecock landed, whether your opponent was forced to move, and whether you were able to transition smoothly into your next shot. Returning the shuttlecock to the backcourt may buy you time to adjust, but it could also give up the initiative; driving it to the net may put pressure on your opponent, but it also requires proper preparation and judgment. Understanding your options through real-life scenarios is more practical than memorizing set answers.

The Third Shot—See How the Two Players Coordinate

The third shot often reveals whether the players have established a front-back or side-by-side division of labor. Observe their racket-holding stance, the distance between them, the open spaces on the court, and their first step after returning the shuttlecock. Positioning changes depending on the shuttlecock’s trajectory and the quality of the shot; it’s not the case that calling “attack” always means front-back positioning, or that calling “defense” always means side-by-side positioning.

Practice Routines for Two Players

Start by limiting the directions of serves and returns; stop after three rallies to discuss. Each player should state one thing: which area they plan to cover and where they hope their partner will return the shuttlecock. Once everyone is comfortable, extend the rallies and gradually introduce different returns. If players repeatedly rush for the shuttlecock or miss it, slow down the pace first and reconfirm their roles.

Notes from the After-Class Session

Choose five rallies and write one sentence for each regarding the direction of the serve, the choice of return, and the positioning for the third shot. When reviewing the replay, verify your observations, note any uncertainties as questions, and then refer to the article on doubles offense and defense rotations on this site. First, explain court positioning and movement, then discuss points won and lost.

Self-check

After returning a serve down the middle, how do you use the third shot to determine which side has the advantage?

Watch to see if the player hitting the third shot can strike the shuttlecock at a high point in front of them, and observe whether their partner can follow up with a net shot or cover the open space. Then, verify this by seeing if the next shot forces them to lift the shuttlecock.
