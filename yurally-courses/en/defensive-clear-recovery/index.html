Clear Shots and Positioning: Using Backcourt Shots to Re-establish Your Position on the Court

D07 · Advanced · Approx. 30 minutes

What You'll Learn in This Lesson

Use a high clear to buy time to get back into position and prepare for the next shot.

Court Practice

  1. Practice a two-shot drill combining a high clear and a net return, for a total of 8 sets. After the first shot, adjust your position before returning the second shot.
  2. Qualification Criteria In at least 5 out of 8 sets, both shots must clear the net, and a preparatory step must be taken before the second shot.

Defensive Skills / Clear Shots and Returning to Position

Practice the positioning, contact, and recovery for the forehand high clear, and use the return as a tool to adjust the pace of the rally.

A High Clear Is About More Than Just Hitting the Shuttlecock Far

A high clear sends the shuttlecock to the opponent’s backcourt and can be used to adjust the pace of the rally and buy time to move into position. This lesson starts with the forehand backcourt clear—which is easy to execute—focusing first on consistency and distance before incorporating movement. Whether a high clear is appropriate depends on the incoming shot, your opponent’s position, and your own abilities; you shouldn’t treat every return as the same situation.

Key Points for Preparation and shuttle Contact

Move early to a position where you can control the shot, preparing with a relaxed grip and fluid body movements. This makes it easier to consistently hit the shuttle in the same spot and avoids having to scramble to make a full-force recovery once the shuttlecock is already behind you. After completing your swing, regain your balance and prepare your grip; don’t just focus on how far the shuttlecock flies and neglect whether you’ll be ready for the next shot.

Drills for Transitioning from Stationary to Moving Positions

Start by having a partner consistently feed you easy-to-handle high shots, aiming for a larger target area in the backcourt; then incorporate short-range positioning drills; and finally, practice moving forward and backward. Play six to eight shots per set, tracking in-bounds shots, backcourt depth, and positioning balance. Adjust the number of sets based on your physical condition; if you make repeated mistakes, lower the difficulty first—there’s no need to swing with full force on every shot to chase distance.

Common Mistakes

If you frequently fail to reach the backcourt, first check whether you’re arriving too late, gripping the racket too tightly, or your movements are disjointed. If you often hit the shuttle out of bounds, leave a safety margin and control the direction of your shots. If it takes several steps to regain your balance after a shot, return to stationary drills at a fixed position; once you can complete your follow-through, gradually increase your range of movement.

Adjust Your Position Based on the Situation

When returning to your position, don’t run to the same fixed spot for every shot. First, assess your return, your opponent’s position, and the possible shot trajectories, then adjust your stance accordingly. In doubles, coordinate with your partner to divide offensive and defensive responsibilities; in singles, stay aware of the space in both the front and back courts. You can expand this to include footwork and movement classifications, practicing shots and movement together.

Self-Assessment After Practice

Can you perform this set of movements consistently? What is the most common mistake? For the next set, what single aspect will you focus on adjusting? Summarize your observations in one sentence, then decide whether to increase the difficulty.

Self-check

After hitting a high clear, your opponent has moved to the back right corner of the court. What should you look for before deciding where to reposition yourself?

First, observe your opponent’s contact point, body balance, and the area you can cover. Move into a position where you can respond to the main shot direction, and take a preparatory step as your opponent swings.
